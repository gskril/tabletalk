The idea.

The organized side of the group chat. Bring friends’ recommendations, shared shortlists, and practical restaurant information into one approachable space.

01

People give it context

Lead with the person behind a recommendation. “Alex went twice” and a considered note are more useful than anonymous popularity.

02

Confident, not loud

Use a strong blue, clean type, and deliberate blocks of color. Keep playful treatments to list covers and the occasional conversational headline.

03

Make the next step easy

Saving, sharing, and comparing should be immediately obvious. The interface should feel as easy to use on a walk as it does on a laptop.

Type hierarchyDesktop headlines 48–64 px; mobile 36–44 px. Section titles 24–32 px. Body 16 px. Buttons and labels 14 px. No all-caps paragraphs.
Spacing and shape8 px rhythm, with compact 16–24 px gaps. 10–12 px corners on collections, 8 px on inputs. A desktop side rail becomes simple mobile navigation.
PhotographyBright, natural restaurant photos with some people and movement. Favor the feeling of being there over polished overhead plates. Keep covers consistent at 4:3.
Motion160 ms button and selection transitions. Use a short confirmation when saving. Skip celebratory confetti and anything that delays the next tap.

Rules for every screen

  • Collections lead with a strong title and a real author. An avatar stack signals specific collaborators, never invented popularity.
  • Use one blue primary action per area. Save toggles have a persistent labeled state.
  • Keep public visit-based people ranking available, with a clear explanation of what the count measures.
  • Separate a public list link from an invitation to edit. Guests should browse without an account.
  • Use visible labels, keyboard focus, and at least 44 px touch targets. Meet WCAG AA contrast for interface text; never communicate state with color alone.

How it
sounds.

Direct, sociable, and lightly witty. Sound like a competent friend who is happy to organize dinner. No forced slang, status flexing, or endless exclamation marks.

Say something like
“Six people. One good shortlist.”

Specific, useful, and comfortable to say out loud.

Leave this behind
“The squad’s new foodie era starts here.”

The people and the places already make it interesting.

Where it
goes next.

First, apply the direction to the working product. Then build the social features that fit it. Stages two and three are proposals, not features already available.

01 / Refine what exists

Make it effortless

  • DiscoverFast photo cards, clear filters, and a few useful collections near the top.
  • NotebookSaved places, Been there, and Lists each have a clear purpose. Saved lists show the author.
  • Review + profileShow where someone has eaten and what they actually thought.
02 / Social next

Build the shortlist

  • Shared list editingInvite-only collaboration, contributor attribution, and simple permissions.
  • List votingA small opt-in “works for me” vote to narrow dinner choices; keep it separate from restaurant ratings.
  • Following activityNew recommendations from friends, with mute controls and no manufactured activity.
03 / Extend carefully

Get dinner organized

  • A dinner planAttach a date and people to a shortlist. Planning first; reservations need a real supported integration.
  • Lightweight invitationsShare a guest-readable plan with an optional RSVP.
  • Group preferencesConsider neighborhood, price, and dietary preferences only when participants choose to provide them.
The tradeoff. Best if shared planning becomes the main reason people return. This is the youngest-feeling option, but the restraint matters: keep the palette controlled and avoid turning the feed into a wall of colorful cards.
