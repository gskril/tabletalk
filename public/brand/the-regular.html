The idea.

A well-kept restaurant journal that gets passed around the table. Taste comes through specific recommendations, considered typography, and people whose opinions you know.

01

Personal, never precious

Use firsthand notes, practical details, and a little wit. The feeling should be “a friend knows a place,” even when the restaurant is special.

02

Editorial hierarchy

Let one restaurant or collection lead. Use generous margins, thin rules, and distinct type sizes to organize a page instead of putting every detail in a box.

03

Warmth from real life

Let dishes, dining rooms, and personal notes bring the color. Burgundy is the signature; the photography supplies the appetite.

Type hierarchyDesktop headlines 56–72 px; mobile 40–48 px. Section titles 28–36 px. Body 16–18 px at 1.5 line height. Labels 14 px; edition metadata 12 px.
Spacing and shape8 px spacing rhythm. 32 px gutters on desktop, 20 px on mobile. Square photographs; 3 px corners on controls. 1 px rules. Minimal shadows.
PhotographyRestaurant-owned photography with a lived-in quality: a dish half shared, a beautiful room, daylight at a counter. Keep the food’s natural color; avoid decorative cutouts or heavy filters.
Motion150 ms color and border changes. No bouncing badges or floating cards. Preserve focus rings and support reduced motion.

From brand
to interface.

Reusable patterns with a consistent hierarchy. Keep the useful actions obvious, with enough personality to make the notebook feel like yours.

Rules for every screen

  • Restaurant names lead; neighborhood and cuisine sit beneath. Put imagery ahead of numeric scores.
  • Show “Visited with Blackbird” as quiet provenance. A visit unlocks a review; there is no decorative achievement badge.
  • Lists look like little editorial collections: title, author, a short point of view, and a photo.
  • Public profiles lead with visited places and reviews. Visit dates stay in the owner’s notebook.
  • Use visible labels, keyboard focus, and at least 44 px touch targets. Meet WCAG AA contrast for interface text; never communicate state with color alone.

How it
sounds.

Assured, observant, and conversational. Write one useful sentence. A little dry humor is welcome; constant dining puns are not.

Say something like
“For the next time someone asks where we should eat.”

Specific, useful, and comfortable to say out loud.

Leave this behind
“Unlock your ultimate foodie adventure.”

The people and the places already make it interesting.

Where it
goes next.

First, apply the direction to the working product. Then build the social features that fit it. Stages two and three are proposals, not features already available.

01 / Refine what exists

The everyday journal

  • DiscoverOne considered lead, then a compact grid with search and Show more.
  • RestaurantLead photograph, useful details, and full reviews. Public lists appear only when they exist.
  • Notebook + profileSaved places, Been there, and Lists. A public profile shows taste through history and writing.
02 / Social next

Pass it around

  • Following activityRecent reviews and public lists from people you follow, with a useful empty state.
  • Collaborative collectionsInvite friends to edit a list; show who added each restaurant.
  • Share pagesAn elegant guest-readable list, with a clear save action after sign-in.
03 / Extend carefully

Taste over scores

  • Personal orderingLet people arrange their own favorites, with context instead of an unexplained global score.
  • Editorial collectionsPublish seasonal, neighborhood, and occasion collections with named authors.
  • Personal suggestionsExplain suggestions through followed people and shared favorites, once there is enough activity.
The tradeoff. Best overall fit. This is the strongest base for the next release: polished enough for the audience, flexible enough for everyday browsing. Keep the serif for hierarchy so dense screens remain easy to scan.
